Home cultures · Sourdough

A sourdough starter, kept simply.

A starter is flour and water holding a community of wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria. You do not need a scale with a hundred settings or a special jar — you need a routine you can repeat and a way to notice what changed.

IllustrationEducational guide, not food-safety certification

What is actually in the jar

Flour carries wild yeasts and bacteria. Add water and the population begins to grow: yeasts produce carbon dioxide, and lactic and acetic acid bacteria produce the acidity that gives sourdough its flavour and helps hold back some unwanted organisms. The community is not one species, and it changes with your flour, water, temperature and feeding rhythm.

That is why two starters can both be healthy and behave differently. A recipe is a starting point, not a specification.

Start one, day by day

Use a clean jar that holds at least twice the volume of the starter. Wholegrain flour is a useful first food because it carries more of the organisms you want, but plain white flour works too. If your tap water is heavily chlorinated, leave it uncovered on the counter overnight before using it.

DayDo thisWhat you may see
1Mix 50 g flour with 50 g water in the jar. Cover loosely.A thick paste. Nothing else yet.
2Keep 50 g of the mix, add 50 g flour and 50 g water.A few bubbles, a faint sour smell.
3–4Repeat the same discard-and-feed once a day.More bubbles, a rise after feeding. It may smell sharp or like nail polish — this stage often does.
5–7Feed once a day, same amounts. Keep it warm (roughly 22–26 °C).A predictable rise and fall, a fresh yoghurt-like smell, and a domed top after feeding.

A starter is ready to bake with when it roughly doubles within four to eight hours of a feed, smells pleasantly sour rather than rotten, and has been doing that for several days in a row. If it is slower, keep feeding and give it time; cool kitchens slow everything down.

Feeding ratios and rhythm

A common starting ratio is equal weights of starter, flour and water — written as 1:1:1. To make a bigger or slower feed, keep the starter smaller relative to the food: 1:2:2 or 1:5:5 will take longer to peak. The ratio matters more than the exact number of grams.

Once the starter is established, a once-a-day feed is enough at room temperature. If you bake rarely, keep it in the fridge and feed it weekly, letting it warm up and peak before you use it. A thin layer of dark liquid on top — sometimes called hooch — is a sign the culture has run out of food, not a sign it is dead. Pour it off or stir it in and feed.

Reading the jar

What you seeWhat it usually means
Even bubbles, domed top, sour milk smellA working starter, ready or close to ready.
Dark liquid on top, no riseHungry. Discard most of it and feed.
Thin and watery after a long pauseOverdue for a feed. It may need a few days to recover strength.
Pink, orange or streaky colour, fuzzy patchesDiscard the whole jar and start again. Do not try to save it.
Strong acetone or decay smellOften a stalled or over-fermented starter. Feed consistently; if it persists with discolouration, discard.

Keeping, sharing and reviving

Keep the jar loosely covered so gas can escape but dust cannot fall in. A screw lid should be left a quarter turn open. Starters can be dried in thin smears on baking paper for sharing or storage, and revived later with water and flour. If a starter has been in the fridge for months, pour off any liquid, discard most of the culture, and feed it twice a day for several days before trusting it to raise bread.

Safety, plainly. This is an educational guide, not food-safety certification. Mould is not a flavour: if you see fuzzy growth or pink/orange discolouration, throw the culture away and clean the jar properly. Do not rely on smell alone to judge a ferment. If you are unsure, discard it — a jar of flour is cheaper than being ill. Nothing here is a health claim or medical advice.

Where this connects

The same habits carry across the counter. Kombucha asks you to watch a pellicle and a pH trend; spirulina asks you to watch light, colour and contamination. All three are covered in the kitchen notes.
