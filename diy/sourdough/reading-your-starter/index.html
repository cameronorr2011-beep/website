Reading your starter.

Temperature and hydration move a starter’s clock more than flour brand does. Learn the two dials and the jar becomes predictable.

The two dials

Hydration. Equal flour and water by weight is 100% hydration and the everyday default. Stiffer doughs (lower hydration) ferment more slowly and tend toward sharper, more acetic character; looser doughs ferment faster and read milder. Ratio changes timing before it changes flavour.

Temperature. Warm rooms speed everything up; a cold bench slows a starter by hours. Treat 22–26 °C as a friendly band for an all-purpose kitchen starter. A jar that peaks in three hours wants a cooler spot or a smaller refresh; one that peaks after ten wants a warmer one or a larger feed of fresh flour.

What “peak” actually looks like

A peaked starter is bubbly throughout, domed on top, smells pleasantly tangy, and has just begun to settle — a rubber-band mark at the starting level will tell you it doubled and held. A starter that only creeps up is usually cold or underfed; one that rockets and collapses is overfed heat plus a rich feed.

SignalLikely readAdjust
Peaks at 3–4 h, sharp smellWarm, loose, hungry base cultureLower hydration or move cooler; reduce starter fraction
Peaks at 8–10 h, domed and fruityCool or stiffWarmer spot or a looser feed (1:2:2)
Flat at 24 h, streaks of liquidHungry — not deadDiscard most, feed 1:2:2, two feeds a day
Grey water and no rise after feedsStalled — feeding again rarely fixes it in placeDouble-discard and refresh; reassess after 3 feeds

A note on honesty

These are tendencies reported across sourdough literature and home practice, not guarantees for your particular flour. Winter flour, chlorine, and water hardness all shift the clock. Record your own timings; that record — not the internet’s median — is your starter’s specification.

Not a diagnosis. Temperature and hydration tune a healthy starter. Fuzzy growth or pink/orange colour is not a “timing” problem — see when to throw the jar out.
