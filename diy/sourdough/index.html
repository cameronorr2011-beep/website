Applications · Fermentation

A sourdough starter, kept simply.

A starter is flour and water holding a community of wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria. You do not need a scale with a hundred settings — you need a routine you can repeat and a way to notice what changed.

What is actually in the jar

Flour carries wild yeasts and bacteria. Add water and the population begins to grow: yeasts produce carbon dioxide, and lactic and acetic acid bacteria produce the acidity that gives sourdough its character and helps hold back some unwanted organisms. The community is not one species — it shifts with your flour, water, temperature and feeding rhythm, which is why two honest starters behave differently.

Start one, day by day

DayDo thisWhat you may see
1Mix 50 g flour with 50 g water. Cover loosely.A thick paste. Nothing else yet.
2Keep 50 g, add 50 g flour and 50 g water.A few bubbles, a faintly sour smell.
3–4Repeat once a day, same amounts.More bubbles, a rise after feeding; a nail-polish smell is common now.
5–7Feed once a day at roughly 22–26 °C.A predictable rise and fall, fresh yoghurt-like smell, a domed top after feeding.

A starter is ready when it roughly doubles within four to eight hours of a feed and repeats that for several days. Slower usually means cooler ambient temperature, not failure.

The short version

  1. Reading your starter — temperature, hydration and the peak
  2. When to throw the jar out — hooch, streaks and mold
Safety, plainly. This is an educational guide, not food-safety certification. Mould is not flavour. If you see fuzzy growth or pink/orange discolouration, discard the culture and clean the jar. Do not rely on smell alone to judge a ferment; when in doubt, discard it. Nothing here is a health claim or medical advice.
