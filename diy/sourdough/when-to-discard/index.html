Applications · Sourdough / Safety

When to throw the jar out.

The complete rulebook fits in one line: if in doubt, discard. Here is what doubt looks like.

Not a problem

Hooch. A layer of dark, alcoholic-smelling liquid on top means the starter ate its flour and is waiting. Pour it off (or stir it in), discard most of the culture, feed, and watch for recovery.

Nail-polish smell at days 3–4. A young starter often smells of solvent before the lactic community dominates. It fades with consistent feeding.

Separation or thinning after a fridge week. Cold neglect weakens a starter; two or three room-temperature feeds usually restore it.

A problem

Fuzzy, dry, coloured growth — green, blue-black, orange or stark white — sitting on top. Mould penetrates, and the culture underneath is not rescued by skimming. Discard the whole jar, wash with hot soapy water, rinse and dry.

Pink or orange streaks or a swirled tinge through the culture. Do not wait for fuzzy tufts. Discard.

Persistent acetone with discolouration. Sharp solvent smell alone usually means a stalled, over-fermented starter; combined with any colour change, it is a discard.

The one rule

Appearance and smell are screening tools, not diagnostics. They cannot certify identity, purity or safety of any ferment. When the evidence is ambiguous, flour is cheaper than illness — throw it out.

Not food-safety certification. These limits are a citizen’s rulebook, not laboratory clearance. Immunocompromised people, pregnant people and people on restricted diets should be more conservative, not less.

Sources

  2. De Vuyst L, Neysens P. “The sourdough microflora: biodiversity and metabolic interactions.” Trends in Biotechnology, 2005.
