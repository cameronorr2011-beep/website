Applications · Fermentation

Kombucha, kept simply.

Kombucha is sweet tea fermented by a symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast (a SCOBY) into a lightly effervescent, acidic drink. The craft is timing and attention, not mystique.

Generated imageEducational guide, not food-safety certification

What the culture is doing

Yeasts convert tea sugars into ethanol and carbon dioxide; bacteria then oxidise that ethanol into organic acids — principally acetic and gluconic acids — producing kombucha’s tang. Two organisms are doing opposite halves of the work, and both are held together in a cellulose pellicle. Analysis of the drink’s composition across studies shows its chemistry tracks fermentation time and ingredients rather than any fixed recipe.

Typical ranges, honestly labelled

DialCommon practice rangeNotes
Sugar~50–70 g per litre of teaFeeds the culture; little remains in the finished drink when fermentation runs long.
TeaBlack or green, 1–2 tbsp leaves per litreTea supplies nitrogen; herbal-only bases starve the culture.
TemperatureRoughly 21–30 °CBelow ~18 °C the ferment slows dramatically; above ~35 °C the culture can be damaged.
AcidityFinished pH typically ≤ 3.5pH at or below ~3.5 is the conventional marker that the ferment stayed dominated by the right organisms.
Time7–10 days, first fermentTaste from day 5; sweetness is your timer.

These are literature-informed typicals, not certified limits. Your water, tea strength and SCOBY history will move them.

Safety, plainly. Kombucha is acidic food, not medicine. Pregnant people, young children, and anyone immunocompromised should be conservative. Ferment in clean glass, never in reactive metal. If a batch grows fuzzy or smells putrid, discard it — do not taste “a little to check”.
