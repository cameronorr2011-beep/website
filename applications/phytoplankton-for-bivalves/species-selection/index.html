Species selection.

Larvae eat what fits and what feeds them. Two criteria rule the choice: cell size and biochemical profile.

The usual suspects

Species (common name)Cell size (approx.)Notes
Tisochrysis lutea (“T-ISO”, golden algae)~2–6 µmRich in DHA/EPA; forgiving to culture; widely used for larvae
Isochrysis galbana (Tahitian golden)~3–8 µmSimilar profile; classic hatchery backbone
Pavlova lutheri ( Pavlova, flagellate)~2–4 µmSmall, flagellated, good for early larvae; complements T-ISO
Chaetoceros calcitrans / C. muelleri (diatoms)~4–7 µmRequire silicate; dense nutrition; watch frustule size vs. larvae
Tetraselmis suecica (green flagellate)~7–12 µmLarger, hardy; useful for juveniles and conditioning broodstock

Sizes are order-of-magnitude and strain-dependent; ask your supplier for the specific strain’s dimensions. Silicate dependence and culture maintenance differ — diatoms are nutritionally excellent but add a mineral discipline.

Mix, don’t bet

A two-species mix routinely outperforms either alone because essential fatty acid and sterol profiles complement; DHA-rich T-ISO plus a robust diatom or Pavlova covers early through late larval stages. Keep at least one culture on standby — single-culture crashes at the wrong week are a classic hobbyist heartbreak.

What “best” cannot mean

“Best” changes with water temperature, shellfish species, larval stage and whether you measure survival, growth or spat yield. No single species wins universally; published hatchery manuals converge on tested mixes of two to three strains, not a magic strain.

Sources

  1. Helm MM, Bourne N, Lovatelli A. Hatchery culture of bivalves — A practical manual. FAO Fisheries Technical Paper No. 471, Rome, 2004. FAO — species tables and rationing.
