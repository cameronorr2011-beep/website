Photobioreactors

What a photobioreactor actually controls

Jul 20267 min readEducational

A photobioreactor is, at its core, a controlled water column. Its job is to give photosynthetic organisms a stable environment while making every relevant variable measurable. That deceptively simple framing hides the hard part: biology responds to all of these variables at once.

The variables

  • Light — the energy input. Too little and growth is slow; too much and the culture can suffer photo-inhibition. Distribution matters as much as intensity.
  • Mixing — keeps cells suspended, prevents self-shading, and moves gas and nutrients. It also subjects cells to a light-dark cycle as they circulate.
  • CO₂ and gas exchange — photosynthesis consumes carbon dioxide and produces oxygen. Managing both is a continuous balancing act.
  • Temperature — sets the rate of every metabolic reaction. Cultures have a thermal optimum and a tolerance band around it.
  • pH and nutrients — define the chemistry of the medium and gate which elements are available to the cells.

Why sensors come first

You cannot control what you cannot measure. A practical reactor therefore starts with instrumentation — optical density as a biomass proxy, plus pH, temperature, light, and CO₂ readings — and only then adds actuation. In our systems, every measurement is validated before it is trusted, and every proposed action passes a safety gate before hardware moves.

The honest version

Even a well-instrumented reactor is not a solved system. Models drift, sensors foul, and cultures surprise you. The value of measurement is not perfect control — it is visibility: the ability to see a culture change before it fails, and to record what actually happened for the next experiment.
