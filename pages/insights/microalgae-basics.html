Microalgae: the organisms behind the technology

Aug 20268 min readEducational

Microalgae are single-celled photosynthetic organisms — plants' distant, much smaller cousins. They capture light, fix carbon dioxide, and grow at astonishing rates, and a surprisingly small number of genera carry most of the biotechnology interest.

Why they matter

Three properties make microalgae compelling. First, productivity: under good conditions they double their biomass in days, far faster than terrestrial crops. Second, metabolism: different strains accumulate proteins, lipids, pigments, and other compounds that industry already values. Third, footprint: they grow in water, not soil, which opens production routes that don't compete with farmland.

The genera you keep meeting

  • Spirulina (Arthrospira) — a cyanobacterium, not technically an alga, known for its protein content and for tolerating highly alkaline water.
  • Chlorella — a green alga widely grown for nutritional products.
  • Haematococcus — famous for astaxanthin, a red pigment that accumulates under stress.

What that means for cultivation

Each genus has its own preferred environment, and the differences matter enormously. Spirulina wants alkaline, warm water; Chlorella prefers a different pH and nutrient profile; Haematococcus grows green, then turns red under stress. A cultivation platform that can measure and control these conditions — light, temperature, pH, nutrients — becomes the common infrastructure underneath all of them.

That is precisely the problem Orr Biologicals works on: making the environment measurable so the biology becomes legible.
