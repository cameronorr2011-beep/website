Not included, on purpose

An air stone. They clog, they harbour biofilm, and a bare rigid tube at low flow does the same job with one less thing to fail.

A mains-powered light or pump. Everything runs from USB. Water and wall voltage in the same experiment is a risk no kit needs to take.

A microscope. A Secchi stick answers the question the student actually has — is it growing? — for a fraction of the cost. A microscope is a lovely second purchase, not a requirement.

Bottled water. Three litres of distilled water is 3 kg of postage. Every household can buy it for about a dollar.

You will also need

Three litres of distilled or RO water — not tap water, because the chlorine in municipal water kills the culture — a USB phone charger, and a spot out of direct sun where the bottle can sit undisturbed for a week.

A plug-in timer costs about $8 and is the single best accessory you can add. Twelve hours of light is not something people remember to do by hand for a month.

Handling: the f/2 concentrate is acidic. Rinse the dropper after use, keep it out of reach of small children, and do not drink any of it. Nothing in the kit is food, and none of it is for human consumption.

The first week

Set-up takes about ten minutes. After that the work is a glance and a log entry, until day seven, when you split and double the culture.

  1. Make the seawater

    Dissolve the salt sachet in 3 L of distilled water and stir until clear. That is 30 parts per thousand, the salinity these organisms grow at in a hatchery.

  2. Fill to 375 mL and dose

    Fill the first vessel to two fingers below the shoulder — 375 mL, marked on the bottle — and add 0.75 mL of f/2, about 15 drops. The headspace is deliberate: a full bottle weeps at the cap.

  3. Add the culture, air and light

    Pour in the whole starter tube, cap it, and run the air tube to the bottom. You want a slow stream of small bubbles, not a rolling boil — too much air strips the carbon dioxide the algae need.

  4. Log day zero, then wait

    Lower the Secchi disc until it disappears and write the depth down. Check it again on days three and five. A culture that is growing gets harder to see through, not easier.

Who buys it

The same box gets bought for three different reasons, and each one asks for something slightly different from a supplier.

Hobbyists & reef keepers

A weekly split gives you more live phytoplankton than most tanks can use, at a cost per litre a bottled product cannot reach. It also gives you a culture you can restart after a crash, which is the failure a bottle cannot solve at 9 p.m. on a Sunday.

Use it within a few days of harvest, keep it aerated, and do not pour culture medium into the display tank — the f/2 is for growing algae, not for the aquarium.

Teachers & students

One kit runs one culture station, and the log is designed to be handed in. The seven-day run covers cell growth, photosynthesis, limiting nutrients and contamination with a live organism and a number to measure.

Teachers ordering five or more: email first and shipping is combined, and the protocol card can be supplied as a printable PDF for the class rather than one card per box.

Working biologists

Before you order

Short answers to the five questions that decide whether a kit is right for you.

Do I need a microscope?

No. Growth is measured with the Secchi stick: you lower a black-and-white disc into the vessel until it disappears and log the depth in centimetres. As the culture thickens the disc disappears sooner. A microscope adds detail, but nothing in the protocol depends on one.

How long does the kit last?

The f/2 bottle covers about 40 weekly splits — roughly nine months, inside its twelve-month shelf life. The salt makes 3 L of seawater, enough for several weeks of splits; more is a few dollars at any store. The culture keeps dividing indefinitely if you split weekly and keep the second vessel.

What happens if the culture arrives dead?

Photograph the vessel within 24 hours of delivery and email it. It is replaced, no argument and no return shipping. Cultures ship Monday to Wednesday so they cannot sit in a depot over a weekend, and orders are held when the destination forecast is above 32 °C.

Can I feed it to a reef tank?

Yes. Nannochloropsis oculata is the green phytoplankton most reef keepers already dose, and a home-grown culture is the same organism at a fraction of the cost of bottled live feed. It is not a substitute for good husbandry, and it is not for human consumption.

Why are there two bottles?

Because contamination, not skill, is what ends a beginner culture. Two vessels at different ages mean a crash in one is an inconvenience rather than the end of the project. Professional hatcheries keep backups for exactly the same reason.
