Home cultures · Kombucha

Kombucha, one jar at a time.

Sweet tea, a warm corner and a culture that turns it sour. Most of the work is waiting, and most of the skill is noticing. Start with a ratio you can repeat, then change one thing at a time.

IllustrationEducational guide, not food-safety certification

What a SCOBY is, and is not

The rubbery disc on top — the pellicle — is cellulose produced by acetic acid bacteria. It is a visible by-product, not the whole culture. The yeasts and bacteria that actually ferment the tea live throughout the liquid. A thin, uneven or missing pellicle is normal; a new one forms as the brew works.

That is also why you can start a batch from strong finished kombucha rather than a fresh disc. The liquid carries the community.

What you need

  • A wide glass jar, clean and dry, holding about twice the volume you plan to brew.
  • Loose black or green tea — around 8–10 g per litre, or two to three tea bags.
  • Sugar — roughly 60–80 g per litre. It feeds the culture; it is not there to make the drink sweet.
  • Starter liquid — about 100–200 ml of finished, unpasteurised kombucha per litre.
  • A breathable cover and a band, a warm spot out of direct sun, and a notebook.

A starting ratio

For 1 litreStarting pointWhy
Water1 litre, boiled then cooledBoiling dissolves the sugar and drives off some chlorine.
Tea8–10 g, steeped 5–10 minutesTea supplies nitrogen and flavour compounds. Longer steeping can taste harsh.
Sugar60–80 gFood for the culture. Most is consumed during the ferment.
Starter liquid100–200 mlCarries the culture and lowers the starting pH.
TemperatureRoughly 20–27 °CCool slows it; hot can harm the culture.

Cool the sweet tea to room temperature before adding the starter. Pouring hot tea over a culture is the most common way to kill a batch.

First ferment

  1. Dissolve the sugar in hot water, steep the tea, then strain and cool.
  2. Add the starter liquid and the sweet tea to the jar. Leave headspace.
  3. Cover with a clean cloth or coffee filter and secure it. Fruit flies are the real enemy.
  4. Leave it undisturbed in a warm, dark spot. Taste from day five with a clean spoon.

Seven to fourteen days is a normal range. Warmer kitchens finish sooner. A batch is ready when it tastes pleasantly sour and no longer sweet — but taste is a rough guide, and a pH meter is better. Many home brewers aim for roughly pH 3.0–3.5 at bottling; below 4.5 is the usual safety threshold cited for fermented foods. Record what you measured so the next batch is comparable.

Bottling and the second ferment

Bottle the finished brew in strong, pressure-rated bottles, leaving a couple of centimetres of headspace. Reserve some liquid for the next batch before you bottle. For carbonation, seal the bottles at room temperature for one to three days, then chill them. Open one bottle over the sink the first time — pressure builds faster in warm weather.

If you bottle while the brew is still sweet, you cannot predict the pressure. Taste and measure before sealing.

Reading the jar

What you seeWhat it usually means
Thin, pale, uneven pellicleNormal. It thickens over successive batches.
A new pellicle forming at an angleNormal. It follows the liquid surface.
Stringy brown strandsUsually yeast. Harmless, though some brewers strain them out.
Dry, fuzzy patches on the surfaceMould. Discard the batch and the pellicle, then clean the jar thoroughly.
Very sharp vinegar with no sweetnessOver-fermented. Still usable as starter liquid; too strong to drink as-is.
Safety, plainly. Mould is not a flavour. If the surface is fuzzy or dry rather than wet and translucent, throw the batch away — do not skim it and carry on. Use clean vessels, keep fruit flies out, and do not bottle into ordinary jars that are not designed to hold pressure. Kombucha contains trace alcohol from fermentation. This page is an educational guide, not food-safety, medical or dietary advice, and makes no health claims.

Next steps

Once the rhythm is familiar, the interesting variables are temperature, tea strength, starter proportion and how long you let the second ferment run. Change one at a time.
