Algae news.

A standing desk for the parts of algae work that keep coming back: feed, light, contamination, pigments and the measurement that decides whether any of it worked. Ten briefs, written to be useful to someone with a flask on the bench.

Titles and links are the PubMed records themselves; the one-line hooks are editorial sentences written from each abstract, not the authors' words.

    Feed

    Live cells still do what dried feed cannot

    Bivalve larvae take particles from suspension, and hatcheries feed them live microalgae because the cell is the ration: size, digestibility and the fatty acid and sterol profile all arrive intact. Chaetoceros, Isochrysis, Pavlova and Tetraselmis are grown in bags, carboys or columns and dosed by cell count. The daily work is not the species list — it is keeping a clean, dense culture on schedule so there is something to dose.

    1. Cultivation

      Light path, not reactor volume, sets the design

      As a culture thickens it shades itself, so reactors shorten the light path with narrow panels, tubes or internal illumination, and mix hard enough to cycle cells between lit and dark zones. LEDs and fibre delivery are established options. Overheating and uneven illumination, not vessel size, are what cap productivity.

    2. Food

      Spirulina's protein holds; its price is the argument

      Spirulina is a cyanobacterium grown in warm alkaline bicarbonate media, then harvested and dried into powder, flakes or tablets. Protein content is high and the cell wall is easy to digest. Taste, colour and cost per kilo decide where it lands in food and feed — not the protein number alone.

    3. Water

      Wastewater algae turn a disposal cost into a harvest

      Algae take up nitrogen and phosphorus as they grow, so a treatment pond can strip nutrients that would otherwise fuel eutrophication. Recovering the biomass as fertiliser, feed or energy is the difficult half. Performance tracks climate, loading rate and whether harvest can be relied on week after week.

    4. Energy

      Biofuels and carbon capture share one bottleneck

      Flue gas can feed ponds and photobioreactors, and the biomass yields oils plus residues. But dewatering and harvesting consume much of the energy the fuel would return, and that is where the carbon arithmetic is decided. Cultivation is rarely the limiting step; separation usually is.

    5. Materials

      Algal bioplastics are a feedstock story

      Algal starch and lipids can be blended with, or converted into, bioplastic films and moulded goods. The pitch is a renewable feedstock; the constraint is consistent supply at a price a manufacturer will accept. Drying and separation dominate the cost long before the material reaches a converter.

    6. Pigments

      The pigment fraction pays for the culture

      Carotenoids, chlorophyll and phycobiliproteins are the high-value fraction. Haematococcus under stress accumulates astaxanthin, Dunaliella beta-carotene, Spirulina phycocyanin. The stresses that raise pigment also slow growth, so the trade is managed deliberately rather than pushed to a maximum.

    7. Measurement

      Microfluidics finds the cell the average hides

      Droplet and microwell devices isolate single cells so growth, lipid and pigment can be measured one at a time. Paired with imaging or sequencing, they expose variation that bulk sampling averages away. The payoff is strain selection and phenotyping; it is not yet a routine cultivation tool.

    8. Nitrogen

      Cyanobacteria fix nitrogen where rice already grows

      Filamentous cyanobacteria carry nitrogenase in specialised heterocysts and reduce atmospheric nitrogen to ammonia. In flooded paddies, Azolla with its Anabaena symbiont has a long history as a nitrogen source. Reliability across seasons and scale is still the open question, not the biochemistry.

    9. Hygiene

      Contamination control is routine, not rescue

      Sterile inoculation, autoclaved vessels and media, filtered air and scheduled microscopy are the core of it. Culling a suspect culture early and cleaning between batches works better than treating a crash after the colour changes. What reads as a nutrient problem is often a persistent contaminant.

    What we are watching

    • Reactor geometries that shorten the light path without giving up working volume.
    • Feed formulations that survive a hatchery tank rather than a lab flask.
    • Cheap, reliable sensing for biomass and contamination — the gap that makes everything else hard to schedule.
