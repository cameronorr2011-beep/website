The strain that forgives.

Every week of the year, the same green cell leaves this bench. Nannochloropsis oculata is not the most nutritious alga in a hatchery — it is the one that survives the week you did not have time for it, which is why it is in nearly every hatchery and every classroom.

18 November 2026Cameron Orr7 min read

It is a small green cell, 2–3 µm across, with no flagella and a cell wall thick enough to make it awkward to digest and easy to handle. That wall is the whole story. It lets the organism tolerate the temperature swings, salinity changes and rough handling that kill a haptophyte, and it is the reason a beginner can keep a culture alive on a desk with a USB light.

Where it belongs in a hatchery

Four jobs, and they are different jobs:

  • Rotifer feed. Rotifers take Nannochloropsis readily at 2–3 µm, and a hatchery running a rotifer line for larval fish or for enriching live prey will use more of this strain than of any other.
  • Green-water rearing. A low, maintained density in the larval tank contributes to water quality and gives larvae a background of small particles, and the strain's tolerance makes it the practical choice for that role.
  • A holding strain. When a batch is in transit or a tank is between feeds, Nannochloropsis keeps longer and revives better than the flagellates.
  • Larval supplement. It is small enough for early veligers to capture, so it appears in mixes as a particle, not as the nutritional backbone.

Where it is the wrong feed

Two honest limits. First, its fatty acid profile is EPA-heavy and carries no DHA to speak of, and DHA is the thing oyster and clam larvae are actually short of through metamorphosis. Feed it alone and you get larvae that survive but do not thrive — slower development, poorer set. This is why every hatchery ration is a mix, and why T-Iso or Pavlova is the base feed while Nannochloropsis is the volume.

Second, the thick wall. Some larvae digest it well; some do not. A cell that passes through a gut intact is a particle that cost money and delivered little. The strain earns its place on hardiness and size, not on digestibility.

If a hatchery wants one strain that will not die on them, this is it. If a hatchery wants one strain to feed everything, that strain does not exist.

Growing it

There is nothing exotic in the protocol, and the numbers are the same ones printed on our own batch sheets:

Inoculate at about a tenth of the final volume — 473 mL into 4.26 L — and the culture is usually ready to split in seven days at 20–24 °C. Below that temperature it still grows, just slower; a garage bench in winter is a 10–14 day cycle rather than a seven-day one, and there is no harm in that as long as you plan the feed around it.

Reading a culture at a glance

Colour is the fastest diagnostic you have. Healthy Nannochloropsis is a deep olive green with no yellow at the surface, and the bubbles clear within a second or two of the air being switched off. Pale yellow-green means nitrogen limitation — split it and feed it. Brown, with a smell, means the culture is dying or contaminated and should go down the drain rather than into a tank.

A film on the vessel wall above the water line is biofilm, not algae, and it is the first visible sign that something else has moved in. Clean vessels, distilled water and a vented but covered cap prevent most of it. Once a ciliate or a cyanobacterial filament is established, no adjustment fixes it: discard, sterilise, restart from a clean starter.
