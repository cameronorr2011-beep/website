A blend is a ration, not a strain.

Larvae and sensitive filter feeders do not eat one cell size and they do not eat one kind of cell. A blend is a mixed ration built for that: more than one cell size, more than one nutritional profile, and enough movement to stay off the bottom. Here is how to use one without overthinking it.

27 November 2026Cameron Orr9 min read

Why mix strains at all

A single strain is a single size and a single nutritional profile. That is fine for keeping a culture alive, and it is why starters are sold as one strain per bottle. It is not fine as the only feed for an animal that is building a shell and a body from what it filters out of the water.

Mixing does three things. It spreads the cell sizes, so a larva whose mouthparts change next week still has something it can catch. It spreads the nutrition, because a golden flagellate and a small green alga do not carry the same fats. And it keeps the water column populated all day: T-Iso swims, and Nannochloropsis stays suspended instead of dropping straight to the bottom.

Blends are mixed the week they ship, so the exact ratio can move with what is growing well. The card in the box carries the ratio for that batch. Both strains are grown separately on f/2 and blended at packing, so one does not outgrow the other in the bottle.

If you are not sure which one to start with: larvae get the Larval Blend; anything with a soft body and fine gill structures gets the Sensitive Filter Feeder Blend. When in doubt, the smaller cells are the safer mistake.

  • Larval Blend — the DHA feed. About three parts T-Iso to one part Nanno. Oyster, clam, mussel and scallop larvae from first feed to eyed pediveliger, and rotifers or brine shrimp being enriched before they go into a larval tank. T-Iso supplies the DHA and the movement; Nanno adds a small green cell that stays suspended.
  • Sensitive Filter Feeder Blend — the small-particle feed. About three parts Nanno to one part T-Iso. Feather dusters, gorgonians, sea apples, and scallops or mussels in holding, where the animal filters slowly and the tank fouls if the ration is too rich.

Be honest about the evidence. A two-species mix is not automatically the best diet in the literature. FAO's hatchery manual is explicit that two or three species including a suitably sized diatom and a flagellate beat single-species feeding, and the best growth in a 2016 geoduck trial came from T-Iso plus Chaetoceros. Neither of those is Nanno plus T-Iso. If you are raising larvae to sell, run the Larval Blend alongside a diatom rather than instead of one — the blend is the flagellate half of the ration, and a good one.

How much to pour

There is no single number that fits every tank, and a number copied from someone else's hatchery is usually wrong for yours. Use the tank as the measuring instrument instead:

  1. Swirl the bottle until the colour is even. Blends settle in an hour on the shelf. Pouring the top off the bottle is mostly water.
  2. Pour enough to tint the water pale green. Stop there. A light tint you can see across the tank is the starting point, not a formula.
  3. Look again after an hour. If the tank has cleared, the animals are eating faster than you are feeding and the next ration can be larger. If it is still green when the next feed is due, halve it.
  4. Feed little and often. Two or three small feeds a day suit larvae better than one big one. Adult filter feeders usually take one feed a day, in the morning.
  5. Write down what you poured. Millilitres and time, once a day. Within a week you have a feed rate that is yours, and it will beat any table.

The failure mode in both directions is easy to see. Too little and the larvae stay small and clear, with empty guts under a scope. Too much and the water goes cloudy, the tank foams, and uneaten cells rot overnight and take the oxygen with them.

Reading the tank

  • Clearance time. The single most useful number in a larval room. Water that clears in under an hour means feed more; water that is still tinted at the next feed means feed less.
  • Gut colour. Larvae with a green or golden line through the middle are eating. Larvae that stay clear are not, whatever the ration says.
  • Foam and film. A ring of foam at the surface, or a slick on the glass, usually means excess feed and dissolved organic matter. Skim it and cut the ration.
  • Smell. Fresh sea. A sour or sulphurous tank is a tank that is digesting its own feed.

Keeping the bottle alive

A blend is a live culture, so treat the bottle like one. Keep it between roughly 15 and 20 °C, out of direct light, with the cap loose enough to breathe. Swirl it twice a day so the cells do not pack into the bottom corner and die there. Do not put it in a fridge — cold and dark is how you make a culture stop, not how you store one.

Use an opened bottle within two to three days. After that the cells settle hard and the culture thins out, and you are pouring a weaker ration than the one you paid for. Order the size you will actually use in a week rather than the size that looks cheaper per litre.

The card in the box

Every order ships with a mini card for each algae in it, and blends are no different: one card per blend, with the strains in that batch, the cell-size range and a starting feed rate on one side. Keep it taped to the shelf above the tank. It is faster than reopening this page with wet hands, and it is the record of what was actually in the bottle.
