Hatchery guide · 10 min

What to feed bivalve larvae, stage by stage.

Larvae do not eat "algae". They eat particles they can capture, and the mix that works at day two is not the mix that works at day twenty. This is the ratio side of that problem: which strains, in what proportion, from the straightened hinge to the pediveliger.

Two numbers decide a ration, and they are not the same kind of number. The first is how much — total cells per microlitre of tank volume, which is a feeding-rate question. The second is in what proportion — how the ration is split between a diatom and a flagellate, which is a capture-and-digestion question, and it changes as the larva grows.

The guidance below is drawn from the FAO hatchery manual (Helm and Bourne, 2004) and the CEFAS laboratory leaflets, because those are the figures hatcheries have worked from for twenty years. Where a number is a starting point rather than a rule, it says so.

The rule that coordinates everything: cell volume, not cell count

Equal cell counts of two species are not equal amounts of food. A Tetraselmis cell is roughly ten times the volume of an Isochrysis cell, so a ration written as "50/50" is meaningless unless the units are stated. The FAO manual resolves this with a cell-volume equivalency table, and it is worth memorising:

Read the last row twice. It is why a three-species diet can look lopsided on paper and be balanced in the tank: 3.3 cells per microlitre of Tetraselmis is the same amount of food as 33 cells of Isochrysis.

The stage table

The FAO manual's starter recommendation for D-stage and early larvae (under about 125 µm shell length) is a mixture of one diatom and one flagellate, in equal proportion by cell numbers. The diatom has a size floor: Thalassiosira pseudonana suits larvae above roughly 55 µm, and Chaetoceros muelleri above roughly 90 µm, because below that the larva cannot handle the chain. Tetraselmis joins the diet once mean shell length passes about 120 µm.

Diet by stage, after FAO (2004) and CEFAS

The proportions are starting points from published practice, not a recipe. The FAO manual is explicit that the ration in cells per microlitre varies with species and with larval density in the tank, so the table is a place to start counting, not a place to stop thinking.

Worked rations from the manual

For early Crassostrea and Tapes larvae, the manual gives a target food density equivalent to 100 Isochrysis cells per microlitre, and notes that it can be met by any of these combinations:

Above 120 µm, the CEFAS three-species mixture that larvae grow well on is 33 cells/µL Isochrysis, 83 cells/µL Chaetoceros and 3.3 cells/µL Tetraselmis. Note how small the Tetraselmis count is: it is there for what it carries, not for bulk.

Turning cells per microlitre into litres off the shelf

Rations are quoted per microlitre of tank volume. One cell per microlitre is 1,000 cells per litre, so 100 cells/µL is 100,000 cells/mL. The arithmetic from there is one line:

Litres of culture = (target cells/mL × tank volume in litres) ÷ culture density in cells/mL

Worked example: a 1,000 L tank at a target of 100,000 cells/mL needs 1011 cells in total. A batch culture counted at 2 × 107 cells/mL therefore supplies 5,000 mL — five litres of culture for one feed. Two feeds a day, and the daily draw is ten litres, which is the number that decides whether you grow your own or buy in.

That last step is the honest reason small producers exist. At five litres a feed, nobody is growing this in bottles; at a 16 oz bottle of starter split weekly, a classroom is.

Why the ratio moves at all

Three reasons, in the order they bite:

Capture. A newly set veliger's velum can handle particles a few micrometres across. A Chaetoceros chain at day two is not food, it is debris that fouls the tank.

Fatty acids. Isochrysis and Pavlova carry the DHA and EPA that early larvae need; diatoms contribute sterols and a different lipid profile. No single species covers the whole span to metamorphosis, which is why every published regime is a mixture.

Digestion and gut transit. As the gut lengthens, the larva can process more cell wall and more silica. That is the point at which diatoms stop being ballast and start being the cheapest bulk in the ration.

What to watch instead of the table

Three observations tell you more than any ration sheet. Clearance — if the tank is still green two hours after feeding, the ration is too high or the larvae are not feeding. Gut colour — a larva with an empty gut at mid-morning is being underfed, whatever the table says. Growth rate — daily shell increment falling off while temperature and density are steady is usually a diet problem, not a water problem.

The practical rule we would give anyone: feed to a measured ration, then adjust on clearance and growth, and write both down. A hatchery that logs clearance and shell length will beat a hatchery that follows a ratio exactly, every time.
