One gallon, set up in an evening.

The kit is eight parts and one living ingredient. Set it up on day zero, look at it for five minutes a day, and you have a gallon of feed by the weekend. These are the amounts for a gallon carboy, written out so you are not converting anything at the counter.

27 November 2026Cameron Orr9 min read

Before you start: clear one square metre

A gallon carboy needs a stable, shaded spot with a power socket nearby and room for the light clamp. Not a windowsill — a south-facing window will heat the water past what the cells tolerate by early afternoon. A kitchen counter away from the oven, a utility bench, or a shelf in a cool room all work.

You also need about 3 litres of distilled or RO water. Tap water contains chlorine, and chlorine is there to kill microorganisms; it does not check whether the cells are the ones you wanted. If distilled water is not available, let tap water stand overnight in an open container and accept a slower start.

A US gallon carboy is 3.8 L. Filling it to about 3.3 L leaves the headspace the culture needs to breathe. Sixteen ounces into a gallon is a heavier start than the 1:9 ratio in the starter guide — closer to 1:6 — and that is deliberate. A gallon cannot hold the 4.7 L that a full 1:9 batch would make, and the denser start is why a kit shows colour in three days instead of five.

Day zero, in order

  1. Rinse everything with hot water, no soap. Soap residue is a quiet killer and it does not rinse out of a carboy easily. Hot water, a bottle brush, and a thorough rinse is the whole cleaning routine.
  2. Mix the saltwater. Put 2.8 L of distilled water in the carboy, add 84 g of marine salt, and swirl until the water is clear. If you have a refractometer, check 30 parts per thousand. If you do not, do not guess twice: mix the same way every time so your batches are comparable.
  3. Dose the f/2 before the culture. Add 6.5 mL of f/2 to the saltwater and swirl. Adding nutrient after the cells means a pocket of concentrated medium sitting against the inoculum.
  4. Pour in the whole bottle. Shake the culture bottle gently to lift the settled cells, then pour all 473 mL in. Do not keep a splash back for later — a half-used bottle ages faster than a batch grows.
  5. Add air. Fit the drilled cap, push the airline through so it reaches near the bottom, and switch the pump on. You want a gentle stream of small bubbles across the whole water column, not a rolling boil. If it foams at the top, turn it down.
  6. Add light. Clamp the LED a hand's width from the glass and put it on a timer for 12 to 16 hours a day. Sixteen is for a fast start; twelve is plenty once the culture is dense.
  7. Write down the date and the colour. One line in a notebook: date, strain, salt grams, f/2 volume, and what the water looks like. That line is the only way to compare this batch with the next one.

Total time: about twenty minutes, most of it spent waiting for salt to dissolve.

The first week, day by day

Day one. The water should look slightly greener than it did an hour after mixing, or the same. Both are normal. Check that the airline has not slipped out and that nothing is sitting in direct sun.

Day two. Take the first Secchi reading. Lower the black-and-white disc until you cannot see it, then read the depth in centimetres and write it down. This number is your baseline, and it only means something compared with your own later readings — not with a number from someone else's bench.

Day three. It should be visibly greener. If the colour is unchanged, check the light timer, the air, and the salinity before you change anything else. Most stalled batches are one of those three.

Day five. Take the second reading. A growing culture gets harder to see through, so the depth should be shallower than day two. If it is identical, work through the checklist below rather than adding more food.

Day seven. Split it. Half the culture into a clean bottle or carboy, both topped up with fresh saltwater and dosed with f/2, and you have two cultures at different ages. The splitting guide has the details.

Reading the tank without a microscope

Three signals are enough for a home bench or a classroom:

  • Colour. Deep and even is healthy. Pale yellow-green at the top and dark at the bottom means the culture is hungry and settling.
  • Secchi depth. Shallower over time is growth. Unchanged over four days is a stall.
  • Smell. Fresh sea or cut grass. Rotten eggs, sour milk or a swamp means something else is growing.

What the kit does not include

A refractometer, a heater and a spare vessel. You do not need the first two to start, and a clean drinks bottle works as the third for the first split. The pump, airline, light, salt, food, carboy and culture are the parts that decide whether the batch works; the rest is convenience.

Keep the mini card that ships with the culture next to the carboy. It has the cell size, salinity, light hours and split ratio for that strain on one side, and what it feeds on the other. On a busy week it is faster than opening this page.
