One tube, two litres, four weeks.

A 50 mL starter is the smallest thing we ship and the one most orders begin with. Here is what is in the tube, how far it actually goes, and the four mistakes that end a starter before it ever doubles.

18 November 2026Cameron Orr8 min read

A starter culture is a seed, not a ration. That distinction is the whole reason it is worth buying one: a batch culture is feed you use up, while 50 mL of dividing algae is feed plus the ability to make more feed without another purchase. Get the first week right and the tube is the last time you pay for that strain.

What arrives is 50 mL of dense culture in a sealed tube — for Nannochloropsis oculata, roughly 2 × 107 cells per mL, packed five to seven days after inoculation so the cells are still in exponential growth rather than stalled. The strain, the count and the pack date are on the label. Everything below is the same arithmetic we use on this bench.

The arithmetic, step by step

The rule that matters is a 1:9 dilution and 2 mL of f/2 concentrate per litre of final volume. Nothing else changes as the volumes grow, which is why a starter scales predictably.

Volumes are working volumes, not vessel sizes. Fill a bottle to about 80% so there is headspace for gas exchange and expansion; a brim-full bottle weeps at the cap and stalls. The f/2 figures assume 2 mL per litre of final volume: 1 mL treats 500 mL, 2 mL treats a litre.

The first week, in order

  1. Make the seawater first. Marine salt at 30 parts per thousand — about 30 g per litre — dissolved in distilled or RO water until the solution is clear. Not tap water. The chlorine in municipal water is there to kill microorganisms, and it does not distinguish between the ones you want and the ones you do not.
  2. Dose the medium before the culture. Add 1 mL of f/2 concentrate to the 450 mL of seawater and swirl. Adding nutrient after the cells are in means a local burn of concentrated medium against the inoculum.
  3. Pour in the whole tube. All 50 mL. A starter kept back "for later" is a starter that ages on a shelf while the batch it was meant to seed runs at half the density it should.
  4. Add air and light. A slow stream of small bubbles from a rigid tube reaching the bottom; 12 hours of light at 6500 K, 12 hours dark. Keep the vessel between 20 and 24 °C. Direct sun through a window will cook a 500 mL bottle by mid-afternoon.
  5. Log day zero. Lower a Secchi disc until it disappears and write the depth in centimetres. Repeat on days three and five. A growing culture gets harder to see through, not easier — that number is the only proof you have that anything is happening.

What kills a starter in its first week

Not skill. Four ordinary things, in the order they actually occur:

  • Chlorinated water. The single most common cause of a culture that never greens up. Distilled or RO only, and rinse the vessel with the same water.
  • Heat. Nannochloropsis tolerates roughly 10–32 °C. Above 35 °C it dies within hours, gel pack or no gel pack. A bottle left in a car or on a sunny sill is a dead bottle.
  • No gas exchange. A sealed or over-filled vessel lets carbon dioxide run out and oxygen build up. Vent the cap, leave headspace, keep the air moving.
  • Contamination. Ciliates, cyanobacteria films or a diatom bloom in a flagellate culture. It arrives from unwashed vessels, from tap water, or from leaving a culture open to the room. This is the one failure you cannot fix by adjusting a setting.

If the culture goes clear and stays clear, it is dead — restart from a backup rather than adding more nutrient. If it goes yellow-green at the meniscus around day four, it is nitrogen-starved and needs a split with fresh medium, not a top-up. If it goes brown or smells wrong, discard it and sterilise the vessel.

When a starter is the right purchase, and when it is not

A starter is the right buy when you need a strain to exist rather than a volume to feed out: restarting after a crash, keeping a second strain as insurance before the busy season, seeding a classroom or a home bench, or beginning a batch you intend to split for months. It is also the cheapest way to try a strain you have not run before, because 50 mL tells you whether it grows in your water and your light before you commit to a litre of it.

It is the wrong buy when you need feed tomorrow. A starter is 50 mL; a hatchery feeding veligers needs litres, and a tube will not cover a tank. That is a batch culture — 500 mL, 1 L or 2 L, grown to your ship date — and above 2 L it is quoted as a standing order because it becomes a scheduling question rather than a shipping one.

The hatcheries that never lose a spawn to algae are not the ones with the biggest cultures. They are the ones with a second tube in the fridge before the first one is needed.

Keeping it going

Split weekly, at about half. Pour half the culture into a clean vessel, top both up with fresh seawater and dose each with f/2 at 2 mL per litre, and you have two cultures at different ages — one ready to use, one growing. Rotate which is which and the line never ends. Skip the split and the culture over-shoots its nutrient, crashes, and takes the week's feeding with it.

Two vessels is not redundancy for its own sake. It is the difference between a mistake costing a split and a mistake costing a spawn, and it is why every kit we sell has two bottles in it rather than one.

Start cultures, 50 mL

Nannochloropsis oculata comes from weekly stock in two to four days; the other five strains are grown to order in three to four weeks. Agar slants are available for labs that need a culture stable at room temperature. Counted and labelled the day it ships, and replaced if it arrives dead.
