Cell size decides the feed.

A first-feeding veliger is roughly 60 µm across and can only capture particles a few micrometres wide. Everything about an oyster ration follows from that one constraint, and it changes as the larva grows.

18 November 2026Cameron Orr9 min read

Hatchery feeding charts look complicated because they are describing three things at once: what the larva can capture, what it can digest, and what fatty acids it needs to complete metamorphosis. Size is the gate. If a cell is too large it is not eaten at all, and no amount of it in the tank helps.

Below is the sequence we recommend when quoting a spawn, with the caveat that every hatchery runs its own water, its own temperatures and its own algal room. Treat it as a starting mix, then let the tank tell you what to change.

The sequence

Pediveliger / set day 12–18 >300 µm Diatoms plus T-Iso; Tetraselmis becomes usable at this size
Spat post-set — Diatoms and Tetraselmis, with formulated feed where the nursery uses it

Ages assume 22–24 °C and a well-fed tank; cold water or a thin ration stretches every stage by days. Sizes are approximate live measurements, and a batch that is behind on one stage is usually behind on all of them.

Why the mix changes

First feed is a haptophyte problem. T-Iso is 4–6 µm, motile, fast to divide and rich in DHA — the three properties that matter most in the first week. It stays suspended with gentle aeration instead of settling into a mat on the tank floor, which is why it is the base of nearly every oyster ration rather than a component of one.

Mid-run is where Pavlova earns its place. It carries DHA and EPA in the same cell. It is slower and less forgiving than T-Iso, so it is normally a share of the ration rather than the whole of it, but it covers a gap that T-Iso alone leaves open.

Diatoms arrive with size, not with age. Chaetoceros muelleri is a 4–6 µm cell in a chain, and Thalassiosira pseudonana is a 3–5 µm centric. Both are silica-walled, and both need silicate in the medium — standard f/2 omits it deliberately, because silicate in a flagellate culture invites exactly the contamination you do not want. Order the f/2 plus silicate variant with the strain, or the culture stalls after two divisions.

Nannochloropsis is volume, not backbone. At 2–3 µm it is small enough to be captured early and tough enough to survive a bad week, which is why it appears in green-water systems and rotifer lines. Its fatty acid profile is EPA-heavy and carries no meaningful DHA, so it supplements a ration rather than carrying one. More on where it belongs.

Setting a ration from the tank, not the chart

Published rations are usually expressed as cells per millilitre of tank water, and the honest answer is that the right number is hatchery-specific: it depends on whether the tank is static or flow-through, how often it is drained, the temperature, and how much the larvae are actually clearing. Two practical checks beat a table.

  • Clearance. Feed, then look at the tank a few hours later. Water that is still visibly green means the ration is above what the larvae are eating; water that is clear by the next feed means you are behind. The target is a tank that clears just before the next ration goes in.
  • The larvae themselves. A well-fed larva has a visible green-brown gut and grows a stage on schedule. A larva with an empty gut in a green tank has a size problem, not a quantity problem — check the cell size before adding more of the same.

Overfeeding is not free. Uneaten algae dies, decays and loads the tank with bacteria and ammonia, and a larval tank that goes cloudy is a tank losing animals to a feed problem. It is better to feed slightly under and feed again than to leave a standing bloom in the water.

No single alga covers the fatty acid profile a larva needs through metamorphosis. Every chart in this industry is a mix, and the hatcheries that set well are the ones that treat the mix as the product.

Buying for a spawn

Two things decide what to order: the date of the spawn and the volume you feed per day. Nannochloropsis is in weekly production here and a 16 oz starter can leave in two to four days. T-Iso, Pavlova, Chaetoceros, Thalassiosira and Tetraselmis are grown to order on a three to four week lead time, so a spawn planned around them has to be planned around that, not around a shopping cart.

For a hatchery running continuously, the practical pattern is a standing weekly order for the volume you feed, plus a 16 oz start culture of each strain kept as a backup. If a batch turns up dead or a culture crashes mid-week, the backup is a split rather than a phone call and a lost set. How to run a starter covers that first week.
