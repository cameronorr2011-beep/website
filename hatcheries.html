Live algae for oyster, clam and scallop hatcheries.

Bivalve larvae are filter feeders with a very short reach. What they can eat is set by cell size and cell wall, which is why hatcheries run two or three strains at once rather than one. We culture those strains, count them, and ship them alive.

What you are feeding

The species on your tanks decide the feed plan far more than any supplier preference does. These are the four we are asked about most, and the strains that usually fit them.

Blue mussel, geoduck and Olympia oyster work is quoted the same way — tell us the species and stage and the strain recommendation comes with the quote. Mixed-strain feeding is standard practice for a reason: no single alga covers the fatty acid profile a larva needs through metamorphosis.

Strains held and grown

One strain is in production every week. The rest are grown to order, which is slower but means you get a culture that is seven days old and dividing, not one that has been sitting in a fridge since spring.

Nannochloropsis oculata Weekly production
2–3 µm · 2 × 107/mL
Thick-walled and forgiving. The strain we would hand someone who has never grown algae, and the one we keep six backups of. Used for green-water rearing, rotifer feed and as a holding strain when a batch is in transit.
Isochrysis aff. galbana Grown to order
4–6 µm · motile
The T-Iso clone that most oyster hatcheries run as their base feed. Rich in DHA, fast to divide, and the reason early larvae survive to day ten. Flagellated, so it stays suspended with gentle aeration rather than settling out.
Pavlova lutheri Grown to order
4–6 µm · motile
Carries DHA and EPA in one cell, which is why it shows up in scallop and mussel programmes and in broodstock conditioning. Less forgiving than T-Iso and slower, so it is usually a share of the ration rather than the whole of it.
Chaetoceros muelleri Grown to order
4–6 µm cells · chains
A silica-walled chain diatom for late larvae and post-set. Needs silicate in the medium, which standard f/2 does not carry; order the f/2 plus Si with it or the culture stalls after two divisions.
Thalassiosira pseudonana (3H) Grown to order
3–5 µm · centric
A small centric diatom that fits early larvae where a chain would be too big. Also silicate-dependent, and the strain most likely to be outcompeted if a culture is not cleaned properly between batches.
Tetraselmis suecica Grown to order
7–10 µm · motile
Large, green and robust — a late-larval and broodstock feed, and a standard rotifer enrichment. Too big for a first-feeding veliger, which is the mistake it gets bought for.

Strain availability, plainly: only Nannochloropsis oculata is on the shelf. Everything else is a three to four week grow-on, and if a culture does not confirm clean under the microscope we will tell you rather than ship it and hope. Plan spawns around the lead time, not around the website.

f/2 nutrient concentrate

Guillard and Ryther (1962) f/2, made here as a single acidic liquid concentrate at 500×. One bottle, one dose, no weighing trace metals on a kitchen scale.

Why liquid and not dry salts

f/2 works because EDTA keeps iron in solution where the cells can take it up. Mix dry salts into seawater and the iron precipitates as phosphate before anything can use it — you get cloudy water and a stalled culture. A single-phase acidic concentrate holds the chelate intact, so the bottle you buy in March still works in November.

It also means the nitrate stays in solution. Sodium nitrate is an oxidiser and a listed explosives precursor; shipping it as dry powder in consumer mail is a conversation with a carrier nobody wants to have. Dilute liquid is not.

Dosing

2 mL of concentrate per litre of culture, added when you make up seawater and again at each split. A 60 mL bottle treats 30 L; a litre treats 500 L. Slight over-dosing is harmless; under-dosing shows up as a culture that goes pale yellow-green around day four.

What “counted before it ships” means

Three checks, on the day of packing, written on the label. It takes twenty minutes and it is the only part of this business that cannot be rushed.

  1. Haemocytometer count

    Four grids, averaged, recorded as cells per mL on the label. A culture that counts below 1.5 × 107 does not ship — it gets held and re-grown rather than sold as a full batch.

  2. Colour and motility

    Healthy Nannochloropsis is deep olive-green with no yellowing at the meniscus. Flagellates are checked for swimming under 100×. A culture that has gone pale is nitrogen-starved; one that has gone brown is on its way out.

  3. Contamination screen

    Any cell that is not the strain is a fail: ciliates, cyanobacteria filaments, diatom chains in a flagellate culture. Failures are discarded, not discounted, and the vessel is sterilised before it is used again.

Packing, timing and heat

Live material fails on the last mile far more often than on the bench. The pack-out and the ship day are the product.

Monday to Wednesday, Priority Mail

Nothing leaves on a Thursday or Friday. A culture that sits in a sorting facility over a weekend arrives clear and dead, and no amount of insulation fixes three days in a metal trailer. Priority Mail runs one to three days; ground service is not used for cultures at all.

Inside the box: the culture at 80% fill in the vessel it grew in, double bagged with an absorbent pad, two layers of bubble wrap, a gel pack separated from the culture by an inch of air, and dry goods bagged separately. Labelled live culture, this way up, open on arrival.

We will refuse a heatwave

If the destination forecast is above 32 °C for the delivery window, the order is held and you get an email, not a box. Nannochloropsis tolerates roughly 10–32 °C; above 35 °C it dies in hours regardless of the gel pack. A day's delay is cheaper than a replacement shipment and a lost spawn.

Below 4 °C the same applies in reverse, with a heat pack instead of a gel pack. Winter shipments to the upper Midwest are scheduled around the forecast, not the calendar.

Live arrival: photograph the unopened vessel within 24 hours of delivery and it is replaced. That is the entire warranty and it is unconditional.

Hatchery questions

The ones that arrive with the first email. Anything else, ask directly — a technical answer costs nothing.

What cell density do the batch cultures ship at?

Nannochloropsis oculata ships at roughly 2 × 107 cells per mL after seven days of growth, which is deliberate. Denser cultures survive shipping worse than dilute ones, so the culture arrives still dividing rather than stalled.

Can I set up a standing weekly order?

Yes. Standing orders are scheduled a month at a time and priced per litre per week, below one-off pricing. Tell us the volume and the day you need it and the batch is grown to that date, not pulled from stock.

How is the culture packed, and what happens if it arrives dead?

Cultures ship in the vessel they grew in, at 80% fill, double bagged with an absorbent pad, inside an insulated box with a gel pack and a red live-culture label. If a culture arrives dead, photograph it within 24 hours and it is replaced.

Do you supply silicate for diatom cultures?

Yes. Standard f/2 omits silicate because it invites diatom contamination in flagellate cultures. For Chaetoceros and Thalassiosira orders we supply an f/2 plus silicate variant, dosed the same way.

Can you supply a strain you do not list?

Often, if it is a marine species we can obtain clean and confirm. Strain isolation and scale-up is quoted per project with a two to six week lead time depending on the source material. Send the species name and what you are feeding it to.
